== Narrative

A cohesive story of how the domain works, independent of any specific software system.

=== Overview

Students living independently go through a continuous cycle of acquiring, using, and replacing household items: they buy food and supplies when they have time and money, store them in their living space, and consume them gradually. When items run low or out, someone must notice and replenish them. In shared households this cycle involves multiple members who must coordinate—or fail to coordinate—about what is needed, who will buy it, and when.

Tracking is typically informal, relying on memory, a fridge note, a chat message, or a quick pantry check. This understanding can diverge from reality: items may exist but be forgotten in another location, backups may be opened without being recorded, and purchases may be duplicated from incomplete information. Waste occurs when items expire unnoticed or duplicates are bought while another instance is still available. Consumption is not stable either—during exam weeks or guest visits items are used faster—and bulk purchases can introduce many instances at once, sometimes with different expiration dates in a single pack.

Some households also keep an informal sense of spending on essentials, remembered loosely or estimated from receipts. When tracked, even imperfectly, spending influences decisions such as buying in bulk for a lower unit price, delaying non-urgent restocks, or choosing cheaper alternatives. In shared households, disputes arise when it is unclear which purchases are personal versus shared or when one member repeatedly pays for shared items. Budget awareness therefore becomes another dimension of household coordination alongside availability and replenishment.

=== Actors and artifacts

==== Actors (domain roles):
* Students living alone (single-member household)
* Students living with roommates/cohabitants (multi-member household)
* Household members who often coordinate replenishment (informal "household manager" role)
* Household members who focus on budget awareness (budget-conscious role)

==== Artifacts used today (domain-visible):
* Fridge notes / whiteboards
* Chat messages (e.g., group chat)
* Notes app lists
* Pantry photos
* Receipts or memory-based estimates

=== Typical domain flow (as observed)

. Household members acquire items (sometimes in bulk) and store them in household locations.
. Items are consumed over time; availability decreases.
. A member checks availability using informal artifacts (note, memory, pantry check).
. If an item is judged low or out (often qualitatively—"feels light", "opened backup"), replenishment is triggered.
. In shared households, coordination may succeed (one person buys) or fail (duplicate buying).
. Some items expire before use; households may discover this late and discard them.
. Members may keep informal awareness of spending; when tracked, it influences replenishment choices and can create shared-vs-personal disputes.
. Membership changes (move-out) require updating who participates and who is informed.

=== Scenario-Based Domain Interactions

==== Scenario 1 — Mateo and the Monthly Pantry Audit

Mateo (see Section 4.2, Persona 1) lives alone while attending university full time and, with a busy schedule, relies on memory to track what is available. At the start of each month he audits his kitchen pantry, refrigerator, and hallway cabinet before shopping to find which item instances are low, expired, or missing.

During the audit he discovers expired yogurt pushed behind newer groceries and realizes he had bought another ketchup bottle despite having unopened instances in a location he forgot to check. He then updates his restock list to include only items that truly need replenishment, reducing duplicate purchases and helping manage limited grocery spending. The scenario shows how incomplete inventory awareness, forgotten locations, expiration management, and replenishment decisions emerge in small households under time and attention constraints.

==== Scenario 2 — Sofia and Duplicate Shared Purchases

Sofia (see Section 4.2, Persona 2) lives with two roommates who coordinate replenishment informally via a group chat and a fridge whiteboard. Before shopping she checks the household inventory, notices paper towels and dish soap are low, and—since they are shared supplies—buys replacements.

On returning she finds that another roommate had already bought bulk paper towels that afternoon but had not communicated it, leaving duplicate instances occupying storage space. The inventory became inconsistent because multiple members attempted replenishment independently from incomplete information. The scenario shows how synchronization failures and duplicate purchases arise in shared households that rely on memory and informal communication.

==== Scenario 3 — Julian and Specialty Inventory Replenishment

Julian (see Section 4.2, Persona 3) is a student athlete on a strict gluten-free, high-protein diet, so he depends on a stable supply of specialty items not always available nearby. While preparing the week's meals he checks the inventory and finds the last instance of his preferred gluten-free pasta was consumed days earlier, with no replenishment, so he cannot complete his plan.

He also realizes several specialty instances bought in bulk earlier expired before use, because quantities and expiration dates were tracked inconsistently and the inventory no longer reflected what was usable. The scenario shows how threshold awareness, expiration management, and replenishment timing matter especially for members with specialized dependencies and irregular consumption.

==== Scenario 4 — Andrea and Lending a Household Tool

Andrea (see Section 4.2, Persona 4) shares an apartment with three roommates and usually organizes household storage. One weekend a neighboring apartment asks to borrow the household power drill. Before lending it, Andrea checks the inventory to confirm the drill's storage location and availability, finds it in a hallway utility cabinet, and updates the inventory to mark it temporarily unavailable.

Days later another roommate looking for the drill while assembling furniture learns from the records that it is currently outside the household. Once returned, Andrea restores it to its location and marks it available again. The scenario shows how household inventory supports temporary item transfers, shared item visibility, and location awareness in collaborative environments.
